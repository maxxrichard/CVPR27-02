\subsection{Realistic batches}
\Cref{tab:main} and \cref{fig:keff} compare \method with published methods and architecture-matched controls under the seven batch settings. The decisive comparison is with the anchor-only control, which differs from \method only by the cross-class coupling $\lambda L_T$ and the presence model. Unanchored transduction collapses as the number of effective classes shrinks, anchoring removes the collapse, and pooling the field adds a further margin that is largest in the very-low and low settings, where absent and rare classes dominate, and vanishes in the all-classes setting as predicted by \cref{prop:special}.

\subsection{Online streams}
\Cref{tab:online} reports the online protocol, where per-class methods drift: in a correlated stream, prototypes of classes that have not appeared for a long time are stale or were fitted to foreign samples. \method keeps absent classes tied to their neighbours through the field and lets the presence estimate follow the stream; the inductive variant (predict, then update) retains most of the gain.

\subsection{Smoothness hypothesis and mechanism}
\Cref{fig:smooth} tests Hypothesis~\ref{hyp:smooth} with labels and independently of any method: the Dirichlet energy of the oracle displacement field on $L_T$ relative to degree-matched random relabellings. Ratios well below one on most datasets indicate that semantically related classes share their displacement, which is the premise of the graph prior; datasets closest to one are those where the gain of \method over the anchor-only control is smallest in \cref{tab:main}. \Cref{fig:embed} visualises the mechanism on the synthetic generator of our unit tests: with $\lambda=0$ absent classes remain at their anchors, whereas the pooled field moves them towards the centroids their images would occupy. \Cref{fig:qual} shows example predictions in the low setting.
